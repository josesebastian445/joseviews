Most security advice for small businesses is about email. But in the UAE, a lot of
business actually happens on WhatsApp and SMS — supplier chats, client approvals,
delivery updates, bank codes. Attackers know that, and WhatsApp and SMS scams in
the UAE are aimed squarely at the people who run those conversations.

There are two attacks worth understanding: smishing, where a text message tricks
someone into handing over card details or a login, and WhatsApp account takeover,
where an attacker steals the six-digit verification code and walks off with the
account.

## How the attack works now

### Smishing: the fake delivery, bank or fine

The message looks routine. A parcel is held and needs a small redelivery fee. A
traffic fine or toll balance is overdue. Your bank card has been suspended. A
government service needs you to update your details.

The link goes to a convincing copy of a courier, bank or government page, usually
built for mobile. It asks for card details, then for the OTP your bank sends —
which the attacker uses in real time to approve a payment or add the card to their
own wallet. Some kits also capture UAE Pass or bank login credentials.

Sender names can be spoofed on some routes, and messages also arrive from
international numbers or iMessage and RCS accounts. A message that appears inside
a legitimate thread is not proof it is legitimate.

### WhatsApp account takeover

The attacker starts registering *your* WhatsApp number on their phone. WhatsApp
sends a verification code by SMS or call to you. Then a message arrives — often
from a contact's already-compromised account — saying "sorry, I sent you a code by
mistake, can you forward it?" Or a fake "WhatsApp support" asks for it to verify
your business account.

Once they have the code, they own the account. Your phone is logged out. They then
message your contacts and groups as you: urgent requests for money, more code
requests, links. Each takeover feeds the next one.

For a business, the damage is not just embarrassment. A hijacked WhatsApp that
clients use to approve quotes or send payment details becomes a direct route to
fraud — the same playbook as [business email
compromise](/blog/business-email-compromise-uae), on a channel with fewer
controls.

## Signs you've been hit

- **WhatsApp logs you out** and says your number is registered on another device.
- **Contacts ask about messages you did not send**, often requests for money or
  codes.
- **You receive verification codes you did not request** — WhatsApp, banking,
  email. Someone is trying.
- **Unfamiliar card transactions** or a card added to a digital wallet you did not
  set up, after someone entered details on a "delivery" or "fines" page.
- **Linked devices** in WhatsApp that you do not recognise.

## How to protect your business

### 1. Turn on WhatsApp two-step verification

This is the single most effective control. In WhatsApp, go to Settings → Account →
Two-step verification and set a PIN, plus a recovery email. Now a stolen SMS code
is not enough — the attacker also needs the PIN.

Do this on every phone that runs a business WhatsApp or WhatsApp Business account,
including the shared one at reception. Store the PINs in your password manager.

If your WhatsApp version offers passkey sign-in, enable that too.

### 2. One rule for staff: codes are never shared

No legitimate person or company asks you to forward a verification code. Not
WhatsApp, not a bank, not a colleague, not a friend who "sent it by mistake". Make
this a rule people can recite, and repeat it in onboarding.

### 3. Check linked devices regularly

WhatsApp Web and linked devices are another route in — a scanned QR code on a fake
page can link an attacker's browser to your account. Check Settings → Linked
devices and remove anything unfamiliar. [QR code phishing](/blog/qr-code-phishing-quishing)
covers the wider pattern.

### 4. Pay fines and fees only through official channels

The practical rule for staff: never pay a fine, toll or delivery fee from a link
in a message. Open the official app or type the website address yourself. If the
fine is real, it will be there.

### 5. Verify payment changes on a second channel

If a supplier or client asks on WhatsApp to change bank details, or a manager asks
for an urgent transfer, call them on a number you already have. This rule also
catches [deepfake voice fraud](/blog/deepfake-voice-ceo-fraud) when combined with
a pre-agreed check.

### 6. Separate business WhatsApp from personal phones where you can

A WhatsApp Business account on a company phone, with a named owner, is easier to
secure and to recover than a client relationship living on an employee's personal
number. When that employee leaves, the conversations stay.

### 7. Reduce the value of SMS codes

Where your bank or software offers in-app approval, an authenticator app or
passkeys, use them instead of SMS. UAE banks have been moving customers away from
SMS one-time codes for exactly this reason.

## What to do if it already happened

1. **If WhatsApp was taken over,** reinstall or open WhatsApp and register your
   number again. You will receive a new code; entering it logs the attacker out.
   If two-step verification was set by the attacker, you may have to wait out
   the lock period WhatsApp shows before you can get back in.
2. **Warn your contacts immediately** — by email, a status update from a
   recovered account, or a phone call — that requests from your number were not
   from you.
3. **Turn on two-step verification** as soon as you are back in, and review linked
   devices.
4. **If card details were entered,** call your bank straight away using the number
   on the card or in the official app, block the card, and dispute the
   transactions.
5. **Report it.** In the UAE, report to the police through their eCrime platform or
   your emirate's police app, and report the scam number to your carrier. Keep
   screenshots of the messages and links.
6. **Check what the attacker could see.** Business chats may contain invoices,
   bank details or documents that make follow-up fraud easier. Warn the affected
   clients.
