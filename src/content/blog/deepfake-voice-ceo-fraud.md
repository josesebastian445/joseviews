Deepfake voice CEO fraud is the old "urgent transfer from the boss" scam with one
difference: the call actually sounds like the boss. The voice, the accent, the
way they say your name. Sometimes it is a video call with a face that moves
convincingly enough on a small screen.

For years the advice was "if in doubt, call them". That advice assumed a voice was
proof of identity. It is not any more, and payment procedures built on that
assumption need rewriting.

## How the attack works now

**Collecting the voice.** Cloning a voice now needs only a short sample. Most
business owners have given plenty away: a podcast appearance, a conference talk,
a LinkedIn video, an Instagram reel, even voice notes forwarded around WhatsApp
groups.

**Doing the research.** The attacker learns who handles payments, who reports to
whom, which suppliers you use, and when the boss is travelling. Much of this is
on LinkedIn and your own website.

**Making contact.** The usual pattern is an email or message setting up the
story — a confidential acquisition, an urgent supplier payment, a government fee
that must be settled today — followed by a voice note or a call to "confirm".
Often it arrives on WhatsApp from a new number with the CEO's profile photo and a
plausible reason: "my phone is with IT, use this one".

**Pressure.** The script relies on urgency and secrecy. Don't tell anyone, I'm
about to board, it has to go before the bank cut-off. Those two levers are what
make an accounts assistant skip a step they would normally follow.

**Video.** Real-time face and voice swapping on a video call is possible with
consumer tools. It works best on short, low-resolution calls with "connection
problems" that excuse the glitches — which is exactly how these calls tend to
go.

The same technique is increasingly used in reverse: a fake supplier or bank
representative calling to confirm fraudulent bank details as part of
[business email compromise](/blog/business-email-compromise-uae).

## Signs you've been hit

Before money moves, the warning signs are about the request, not the voice:

- A payment request arriving on a channel the person does not normally use for
  payments, or from a new number.
- Secrecy: an instruction not to involve the usual approvers.
- Urgency tied to a deadline you cannot verify.
- A refusal or excuse when you offer to call back on their known number.
- A beneficiary you have never paid before.

Some fakes have audible tells — flat emotion, odd pauses, a slightly wrong
rhythm. Do not rely on spotting them. The technology improves faster than people
learn to hear it, and a stressed employee on a bad line will not notice.

After money moves, the sign is simple: the real executive has no idea what you are
talking about.

## How to protect your business

The fix is procedural, not technical. You cannot reliably detect a deepfake, so
you design a process where a perfect deepfake still cannot move money.

### 1. Verify through a second channel you initiate

Any payment request or bank detail change made by voice, video or message is
confirmed by calling the person back on a number already on file. You hang up,
you dial. Not the number they called from, not one they give you.

If the request is genuine, this costs two minutes. If it is not, it ends the
attack.

### 2. Dual approval that cannot be overridden by seniority

Payments above a set amount need two people to approve in the banking portal.
The rule has to apply when the instruction comes from the CEO — especially then,
because that is exactly who will be impersonated. The CEO should say so,
explicitly, to the finance team.

### 3. A verbal challenge for leadership

Agree a challenge phrase or question between executives and the people who
process payments, known only to them and never written in email or chat. It
sounds old-fashioned. It works against cloned voices because the attacker
cannot know it.

### 4. Payments only through the normal process

No payment is made because of a call or a voice note alone. Every payment
needs an invoice or written request in the normal system, and the approval
happens in the bank portal with its own MFA. The call is never the authorisation.

### 5. Brief the people who move money

A fifteen-minute conversation with your accounts team about this specific scam
is worth more than generic security training. Tell them, in your own voice: "If
I ever ask you to skip the process, assume it is not me." Then thank anyone who
challenges you.

### 6. Secure the accounts that set up the story

Many of these attacks start with a compromised mailbox or a hijacked WhatsApp
account. MFA on email, two-step verification on WhatsApp, and SIM swap
protection on executive numbers all reduce how convincing the setup can be — see
[SIM swap attack protection](/blog/sim-swap-attack-protection) and
[WhatsApp and SMS scams in the UAE](/blog/whatsapp-sms-scams-uae).

## What to do if it already happened

In order:

1. **Call your bank immediately** and request a recall of the transfer. Ask
   them to contact the receiving bank. Time is the biggest factor in recovery.
2. **Report to the police** through the UAE eCrime platform or your local
   police station, and keep the reference number for your bank and insurer.
3. **Preserve evidence.** Keep the voice notes, call logs, numbers, messages
   and emails. Do not delete the WhatsApp chat.
4. **Check whether an account was compromised.** If the setup email came from
   a real internal mailbox, treat it as an account takeover: reset, revoke
   sessions, check inbox rules.
5. **Tell your cyber insurer** if you have cover. Check whether social
   engineering fraud is included; it is often a separate clause.
6. **Review, do not blame.** The employee followed what sounded like a direct
   instruction. Fix the process that let one person act on it alone.

## What this doesn't cover

This post is about impersonation for payment fraud. It does not cover deepfakes
used for reputational harm, fake job applicants, or identity verification bypass
on onboarding systems, which are separate problems with separate fixes.
